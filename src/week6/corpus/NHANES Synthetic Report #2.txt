Dietary Intake (24‑Hour Recall)

Executive Summary

This synthetic NHANES dietary intake report summarizes nutritional patterns collected through a 24‑hour dietary recall interview. The dataset includes 10,000 respondents and reflects realistic U.S. consumption trends. The report covers total caloric intake, macronutrient distribution, food group consumption, nutrient adequacy, and demographic differences.

Key findings include high intake of added sugars, low fiber consumption, and persistent disparities in diet quality across socioeconomic groups.

1. Survey Description

The NHANES 24‑hour dietary recall uses the Automated Multiple‑Pass Method (AMPM), a structured interview designed to improve accuracy of self‑reported food intake. Respondents report all foods and beverages consumed from midnight to midnight the previous day.

This synthetic dataset includes:

10,000 respondents

All ages

Oversampling of minority groups

English and Spanish interviews

Detailed nutrient profiles generated using USDA food composition tables (synthetic values)

2. Total Energy Intake

Average daily caloric intake varies by age and sex.

Adults (20+ years):

Mean intake: 2,080 kcal

Men: 2,350 kcal

Women: 1,820 kcal

Children (2–19 years):

Mean intake: 1,650 kcal

Interpretation:  
Adults consume more calories than recommended for weight maintenance, especially men aged 20–39. Children’s caloric intake aligns with expected growth patterns.

3. Macronutrient Distribution

Percent of total energy intake:

Carbohydrates: 49%

Protein: 17%

Total fat: 34%

Saturated fat: 11%

Narrative Analysis:  
Carbohydrate intake is within recommended ranges, but saturated fat exceeds dietary guidelines. Protein intake is adequate across all demographic groups.

4. Food Group Consumption

Daily average intake:

Fruits: 0.8 cups

Vegetables: 1.3 cups

Whole grains: 0.9 ounces

Refined grains: 5.1 ounces

Dairy: 1.4 cups

Added sugars: 17 teaspoons

Interpretation:  
Fruit and vegetable intake remains below recommended levels. Added sugar consumption is significantly higher than dietary guidelines, especially among adolescents.

5. Nutrient Adequacy Indicators

Percent of respondents below recommended intake:

Fiber: 72%

Vitamin D: 64%

Iron (women 20–49): 22%

Calcium: 41%

Potassium: 68%

Narrative Analysis:  
Fiber and potassium deficiencies are widespread. Vitamin D intake is low across all age groups, reflecting limited consumption of fortified foods and low sun exposure.

6. Dietary Patterns by Income

Income‑to‑poverty ratio (IPR) strongly influences diet quality.

Low‑income respondents (IPR < 2.0):

Higher consumption of refined grains

Lower fruit and vegetable intake

Higher added sugar intake

Lower dairy consumption

Higher‑income respondents (IPR ≥ 2.0):

More whole grains

Higher fruit and vegetable intake

Lower sugar‑sweetened beverage consumption

Interpretation:  
Socioeconomic disparities in diet quality persist and mirror national trends.

7. Age‑Related Dietary Differences

Children and adolescents:

Highest added sugar intake

Lower vegetable consumption

Higher snack food consumption

Older adults (60+):

Lower total caloric intake

Higher fruit consumption

Lower sodium intake

8. Key Findings

Added sugar intake exceeds recommendations for most respondents.

Fiber intake is low across all demographic groups.

Socioeconomic status strongly predicts diet quality.

Adolescents show the poorest dietary patterns.

Older adults have healthier macronutrient distribution but lower protein intake.

9. Conclusion

This synthetic NHANES dietary intake report provides a realistic representation of U.S. nutritional patterns. The findings highlight persistent dietary challenges, including low fruit and vegetable intake, high added sugar consumption, and widespread nutrient inadequacy. These patterns provide a strong foundation for downstream modeling, health risk assessment, and NLP corpus development.
